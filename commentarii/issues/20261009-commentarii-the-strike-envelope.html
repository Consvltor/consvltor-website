CommentariiIssue 0259 October 2026 TMT & Digital Infrastructure

The Strike Envelope

Ukraine disabled Yandex’s data centre at Sasovo on 8 October and part of its Kaluga site on 9 October, after Russia’s defence ministry said on 6 October that it had struck four Ukrainian data centres. Yemeni government forces said they had captured positions near the Bab el-Mandeb strait, through which most Europe-Asia cable capacity passes. Greece said it would not seek Turkish consent to survey the rest of the Great Sea Interconnector route, and Finland ordered Google to suspend site preparation at Muhos and Kajaani by 23 October. A strike envelope is the area within reach of a given weapon, and the attacks on compute raise the question of whether workloads can continue from facilities outside the same regional strike exposure.

Macro and Geopolitical Landscape

The US Energy Information Administration (EIA) raised its 2026 Brent forecast to about $96 a barrel on 6 October, with a fourth-quarter forecast of about $105.

The October Short-Term Energy Outlook was released on 6 October, and its fourth-quarter forecast is $14 a barrel above last month’s outlook.9 The EIA finalised its inputs on 1 October, and the outlook states that it does not account for additional supply from the Group of Seven announcement of 2 October covered in Issue 024. For digital infrastructure, higher oil prices feed through to the cost of backup diesel and to contractor escalation clauses, and they would not translate mechanically into data-centre electricity tariffs, which depend on local market structure.

The Reserve Bank of India raised its repo rate by 25 basis points to 5.50% on 7 October, its first rise since February 2023, according to financial news reports.10 The effect on rupee borrowers depends on their benchmark, reset terms and hedging. The euro reached a 17-month low against the dollar on 5 October, which affects dollar-reporting investors with euro revenue through translation.11 The European Central Bank raised its deposit facility rate to 2.50% on 10 September.12

Reciprocal Targets

Ukraine disabled Yandex’s Sasovo data centre on 8 October and part of its Kaluga site on 9 October, after Russia’s defence ministry named four Ukrainian data centres as targets on 6 October.

The Russian defence ministry said on 6 October that it had struck the Parkovyi, Ukrtelecom, Giga and Podolskyi data centres, with the stated aim of reducing Ukraine’s communications capabilities, a claim not independently confirmed.13 Parkovyi, in Kyiv, reported on 1 October that its facility was critically damaged and had stopped operating, and that it had moved customer data to backup sites in the European Union.14 Data Center Dynamics lists eight Ukrainian operators with damaged facilities in recent weeks, including Vodafone Ukraine, Cosmonova and Ukrtelecom, and notes that it is not known whether they are all separate sites.1516

Ukraine then struck Yandex’s data centre at Sasovo, in Ryazan Oblast, on 8 October. Yandex said the strike caused a fire that forced the site to cease operations, and the Yandex Cloud status page recorded a power outage in one availability zone. Data Center Dynamics described it as the first major data centre taken down in Russia during the conflict.15 On 9 October Yandex said that several modules at its Kaluga data centre were disabled.1718

Sasovo housed two of Yandex’s three Nvidia A100-based supercomputers according to the company’s earlier disclosures, and Yandex has not confirmed whether those systems were damaged. A loss of AI training capacity should not be treated as established.15

Separate sites can share exposure in two ways. A regional strike campaign can reach several facilities in the same area, and a shared power supply or network link can be a single point of failure for facilities that look independent. Operators and investors with positions in or near the conflict could test how much contracted revenue would continue from replicas outside the affected region, how quickly workloads can move there and whether the replicas rely on the same power or network links. Force majeure wording in compute contracts may also warrant review.

§6

Yemen’s Offensive Reaches Bab el-Mandeb

Yemeni government forces said they had seized positions near the Bab el-Mandeb strait and taken Mocha, claims the Houthis dismiss and Al Jazeera could not independently verify on 6 October.

The positions include Mocha and Dhubab in Taiz governorate, and Yemen’s defence ministry later said Mocha had fallen. A Houthi military spokesman said the group still controls its battlefield gains and called the reports fabricated.6

The underwriting question is whether a repair ship can safely reach a damaged cable, in addition to whether a cable fails. Doug Madory of Infoblox told Rest of World that a repair might take one to two weeks depending on the availability of a ship, while the last cuts in this waterway took months because the repair ships needed assurances they would not be fired upon.19

Rest of World reports that more than 90% of data capacity between Europe and Asia runs through cables under the Red Sea, and describes Bab al-Mandab as a strait less than 32 kilometres wide.19 Citing a person familiar with the deal who asked not to be named, Rest of World reports that Google paid a premium in September for a pair of fibre strands along Turkish state pipelines. That single account does not provide a like-for-like price benchmark for other routes. The report also says that Google and Meta have begun routing some live traffic through a land route across Iraq that they had previously kept in reserve, and neither company responded to requests for comment.19 Iraq signed a contract with Ooredoo on 10 September for an underground route from Al-Faw, and Microsoft said on 23 September that it plans to invest more than $400m in subsea and land links across the Middle East by 2030.19

Route concentration at one narrow strait, the safety conditions for repair ships and the price and delivery risk of land alternatives are the variables an operator could act on. Cable investors may wish to test repair-time assumptions that include safe access, and the share of capacity with a tested terrestrial alternative. Rest of World describes no new cable fault.

§7

The Unsurveyed Stretch

Greece will not seek Turkish consent for surveys of the stretch of the Great Sea Interconnector route between Kasos and Cyprus, a stretch in waters both countries claim.

ProtoThema reported on 5 October that Athens will not seek Turkish permission for the remaining surveys of the Great Sea Interconnector, the planned Crete-Cyprus power cable, and that the project will proceed. Greek City Times also quoted Foreign Minister Giorgos Gerapetritis calling any attempt to obstruct the project inconceivable.2021 ADMIE, Greece’s grid operator, says about 60% of the required surveys are complete. The outstanding surveys lie between Kasos and Cyprus, in waters where Greece and Turkey have competing maritime claims, and ADMIE is finishing preparations to resume them while it discusses with the government when to request a survey vessel and issue a navigational warning to shipping (NAVTEX), with no dates given.21

Turkey’s NAVTEX 0953/26, valid to 25 October, covers an Oruç Reis survey of a proposed gas pipeline route between Turkey and northern Cyprus, as covered in Issue 024. That survey is on a different route from the interconnector surveys.21

The Great Sea Interconnector is a power cable, so its read-through for telecom cable owners is narrower. A data-cable project crossing the same disputed waters would also need to survey and lay cable along its intended route without agreement between the two states. Sponsors and lenders could test schedules against that dependency and read contracts for delay and standoff clauses.

§8

Finland’s Stop Order

Finland’s Licensing and Supervision Agency directed Google’s local entity to suspend significant site preparation at Muhos and Kajaani by 23 October.

Tuike Finland Oy, Google’s local entity, must suspend significantly environment-altering preparatory work at its planned data-centre projects in Muhos and Kajaani until the environmental impact assessments are complete, the Licensing and Supervision Agency (LVV) said in a press release on 6 October. The suspension covers forest clearance, earthworks, drainage and access infrastructure, and the agency says extensive preparatory measures have already been carried out.22 Tuike must give a written explanation by 14 October and the suspension must take effect no later than 23 October, and the agency is considering enforcement, possibly with a conditional fine, if the company does not comply.22 Capacity reports more than 300 hectares of forest cleared across the two sites.23

The order is environmental enforcement against one company, and the reporting describes no sovereignty dispute and no action aimed at US technology investment as such. Google said it fell short of its own standards and had acted in good faith under the Forestry Act, and Finland’s environment minister said projects would likely be delayed.24 The sites form part of the €13bn Finnish programme announced in September.25

On 8 October the French group Sesterce announced an investment of over €10bn in a data-centre campus at the former Kaipola paper mill in Jämsä. The first phase of 200 MW has works planned to start in 2026, capacity is expected to rise to 600 MW in a second phase with no date given, and Sesterce has an anchor customer it has not named. Over the longer term it aims for more than one gigawatt of AI capacity in Finland, in line with market demand.26 Those are announced plans, and the campus is not yet operational capacity.

Announced investment does not show that an environmental assessment is complete or that a delivery schedule is secured. Investors in European compute could ask what spend and delivery commitments are already exposed at a site if an assessment requires redesign or a long suspension, and whether brownfield plots such as former industrial sites carry lower permitting risk than cleared forest.

Strategy Actions

Immediate · 0 to 90 days
  • Site power autonomy, restoration time and strike-envelope overlap for data centres near conflict zones could be tested now. Yandex lost power in one availability zone after a single strike.
  • Repair-ship access and terrestrial alternatives for Red Sea capacity may warrant review. The last Red Sea repairs took months because ships needed safety assurances.
  • Pending compute sites could confirm the status of their environmental assessments and the spend already committed before land works begin. LVV’s stop date for Google’s two sites is 23 October.
Platform · 3 to 12 months
  • Schedules for cable projects in contested waters could model survey-consent disputes and vessel standoffs. Turkey’s survey notice runs to 25 October and Greece is preparing to resume its surveys.
  • Force majeure and termination terms in GPU cloud contracts would merit review against acceptance deadlines. The Cohere agreement allows termination and a refund if the minimum infrastructure is not accepted by 15 July 2027.
Portfolio · 12 to 24 months
  • UK fibre exposure could be weighed against the CMA’s 15 December decision on nexfibre and Substantial and its review of BT/TalkTalk. Either could change which platforms are available as acquirers or targets.
