Sentences, Bills & Strikes

Helsinki District Court sentenced the captain of the cargo ship Fitburg to three years and six months in prison on 5 November, and its bosun to two years and ten months, for aggravated sabotage over damage to cables in the Gulf of Finland. On 3 November the United States struck a Revolutionary Guard air-defence site on Iran’s Qeshm island and Russia attacked Ukraine’s energy facilities more than 20 times in 24 hours. Taiwan’s legislature heard a bill on 2 November that would make unlicensed chip exports to China punishable by up to 10 years in prison.

The title takes one word from each thread: sentences, for the Finnish court’s custodial terms; bills, for the Taiwan amendment; and strikes, for the attacks on an Iranian air-defence site and on Ukraine’s power stations. In each, a state body decides whether infrastructure is protected, so the exposure for investors sits with that decision as well as with the asset.

Macro and Geopolitical Landscape

Brent crude fell more than 8% to about $94 intraday on 4 November after the US midterm elections and settled at $98.29 on 5 November, and the Reserve Bank of Australia raised its cash rate to 4.85% on 3 November, citing fuel and commodity inflation from the Middle East conflict.

Brent fell more than 8% to about $94 around midday on 4 November, an intraday level rather than a close, after the US midterm results, according to CBS News, and Fox News put the settlement at $98.29 on 5 November.34 The move came in the same week as diplomacy through Qatar and a US strike on Qeshm, and Al Jazeera quoted analysts who flagged a risk of strikes by a lame-duck administration.5

Qeshm Strike, Hormuz Attacks and Qatar-Mediated Talks

The United States struck an Iranian Revolutionary Guard air-defence site on Qeshm island on 3 November, and a week of reported tanker attacks near the Strait of Hormuz ended with Fox News reporting that Trump’s national security team was weighing wider operations.

Trump confirmed that US forces struck a Revolutionary Guard air-defence site on Qeshm island on 3 November after the Guard fired at a US aircraft, and described the strike as minor, while Iran’s leadership called Washington’s reply to its Hormuz proposal unconvincing.1 Fox News, citing Axios, reported on 6 November that Trump met his national security team on 5 November on resuming major operations, that US Central Command was finalising plans and that no directive had been given.4

The tanker attacks carry different evidential weight depending on who reports them. The Guard’s navy said on 2 November, through Tasnim, that 11 tankers had caught fire in nine days, a claim the outlet carries without independent confirmation.9 The UK Maritime Trade Operations centre (UKMTO) is a more neutral record: Fox News reported that it had logged five attacks since Sunday 1 November, and CBS News reported a UKMTO-recorded projectile strike on a tanker about 50 nautical miles north of Madinat ash Shamal in Qatar on 5 November.43

Diplomacy ran in parallel through Qatar. Iranian negotiators were in Doha for a third day of talks on 2 November, and Iran’s foreign minister described the indirect exchanges with Washington as intensive on 3 November.1011 Al Jazeera reported on 4 November that Iranian leaders read the US midterm results as a rejection of the war, and quoted a Chatham House analyst who expects an interim Hormuz arrangement to be more realistic than a full settlement.5

Al Jazeera also reported on 4 November, citing Iranian officials, that Gulf undersea cables and European countries could be targets if Iran is attacked; this is a single-outlet report and no cable damage is cited in it.1 For operators and investors with Gulf data-centre, landing-station or cable exposure, the near-term variables are whether Washington decides on wider strikes in the coming days, whether the Qatar-mediated track produces an interim Hormuz arrangement, and whether business-interruption cover, force majeure clauses and repair-vessel terms assume a conflict that has already resumed.

Ukraine’s Grid and the Abu Dhabi Talks

Ukrenergo reported more than 20 attacks on energy facilities in 24 hours on 3 November, with energy facilities attacked in nine regions and emergency outages in Kyiv, and Zelensky said that day that Russian, Ukrainian and US negotiators would meet in Abu Dhabi on 10 and 11 November.

The attack began overnight and was still under way on the afternoon of 3 November, when Ukrenergo said the situation in the power system had worsened and emergency outages were in force in several regions, including Kyiv.12 The government’s later update said generation and transmission sites in nine regions were struck overnight, and the wider assault caused damage in 14 regions in total, including large power plants in the Dnipropetrovsk, Vinnytsia and Kharkiv regions and several substations, some in western Ukraine, and thousands of power workers were restoring supply with critical infrastructure first.12 The report does not mention telecom effects.

Zelensky said at a press conference in Berlin on 3 November that the Abu Dhabi talks would cover security guarantees, an energy ceasefire and a Black Sea truce, and that it would be the first direct three-way meeting since February.2 A week earlier, on 28 October, he had said the proposed energy ceasefire was not working because Russia had not reciprocated.13 On 5 November Trump separately met Kirill Dmitriev, Putin’s envoy, at the White House, and said a larger agreement being worked on would involve more than Ukraine, according to PBS.14

For operators and investors with Ukrainian telecom, data-centre or tower assets, the strike on 3 November reached the same grid that powers their sites, so battery autonomy, generator fuel and the rotation schedule of emergency outages decide availability more than any ceasefire headline. An energy-only ceasefire, if agreed, would address the risk to power-dependent assets directly, and on 28 October Zelensky said Russia had not reciprocated the earlier proposal.

The Fitburg Verdict: Baltic Cable Damage

Helsinki District Court sentenced the Fitburg’s captain to three years and six months and its bosun to two years and ten months on 5 November, the first conviction in Finland’s two Baltic anchor-dragging cable cases, and the ruling is not final.

The court found the captain and the bosun guilty of aggravated sabotage, concluding that the Fitburg dragged its anchor chain and anchor stock along the seabed for about 130 kilometres over several hours outside Finnish territorial waters, crossing nine telecommunications and electricity cables connecting Finland with other countries and a gas pipeline, and that both men knew the anchor was dragging.15 The three judges were unanimous after eight hearing days between September and October, and both men were ordered into custody immediately.15 The damage occurred in the Gulf of Finland on 31 December 2025; two telecommunications cables were damaged and the remaining infrastructure was put at risk.15 The court ordered the two men to pay about €2 million in compensation for damage and investigation costs, according to Helsingin Sanomat as relayed by Helsinki Times; the order is against the convicted crew members, and the report does not mention any liability for the shipowner.15 An order to pay is also different from collection, and the ruling is not final.

Jurisdiction was the contested point. Prosecutors argued that the damage occurred in Estonia’s exclusive economic zone with some in Finnish waters, and the defence argued Finland could not prosecute.16 The same court dismissed the Eagle S case for lack of jurisdiction in October 2025, and the Court of Appeal in August 2026 held that Finland has jurisdiction in that case and returned it for trial.1718 In this case the court rejected the argument, finding that the intentional offences had consequences in Finland.15 Prosecutors had asked for at least two years and six months, so both sentences are above that minimum.16

The Fitburg verdict is not the first conviction for cable damage anywhere: a Taiwanese court sentenced the captain of the Hong Tai 58 to three years in June 2025 for cutting a cable near Penghu.19 It is the first in Finland’s Baltic cases, and it rests on a finding of knowledge that the defendants dispute. For cable owners and insurers in the Baltic, a custodial sentence raises the personal cost to a crew of dragging an anchor. It does not shorten a repair or establish that damage is recoverable from a vessel owner. An appeal could take the jurisdiction and knowledge findings to a higher court, and recovery terms in landing and maintenance agreements may be worth reading against that timeline.

Taiwan: Chip Export Bill and Seabed Mapping

Taiwan’s legislature heard a Trade Act amendment on 2 November that would punish unlicensed exports of specified chips and chipmaking equipment to China, including shipments routed through a third country, with up to 10 years in prison, and the Wall Street Journal reported that China has expanded seabed mapping east and south of Taiwan.

The amendment, proposed by lawmakers of the Democratic Progressive Party, would ban exports of specified integrated circuits, key wafer materials, components and chipmaking equipment to mainland China without government approval, with penalties of up to 10 years in prison and fines of NT$5 million to NT$10 million (about US$155,000 to US$310,000) and with the same penalties for shipments routed through a third country.20 William Liu, director-general of the International Trade Administration, told the hearing that export controls have become a strategic tool for protecting a technological edge, and said the ministry is studying a fuller package.20 The opposition Kuomintang and Taiwan People’s Party hold a legislative majority and have not stated positions, so the bill could stall, and the government’s alternative is to add AI servers and advanced chips to the control list by public notice, which would not carry the bill’s higher maximum sentence, third-country coverage or mandatory confiscation.20

The bill follows a case that exposed the gap. Keelung prosecutors indicted nine people in August, including the general manager of Flying Tiger Technology and staff at the local Nvidia and Super Micro units, over the alleged diversion of Supermicro servers fitted with Nvidia GPUs to Chinese buyers, with alleged profit of more than NT$600 million (about US$18.5 million), reportedly via Indonesia, Japan and Hong Kong; no one faces an export charge, because prosecutors used breach of trust and forgery charges based on false end-user papers.2021

The Wall Street Journal reported, in a piece relayed by Taiwan News on 1 November, that China has expanded undersea mapping east and south of Taiwan, with surveying increasing after Japan and the Philippines announced in May that they would hold talks on overlapping maritime boundaries east of Taiwan.22 Bryan Clark of the Hudson Institute said the surveys help Chinese submarines and underwater drones and that research vessels are also used to locate telecommunications cables, which could let Beijing cut Taiwan off from outside communications in a coercion campaign, and a Center for Strategic and International Studies report cited by the Journal found that China-linked research vessels spent a disproportionately large share of their observed time near critical infrastructure compared with vessels from 47 other countries.22

For buyers of AI servers and operators of data centres that source hardware through Taiwanese channels, the practical question is whether the control list takes effect by notice before the bill passes, because liability for diversion through a third country would put end-user documentation and reseller chains under criminal scrutiny. For owners of cables landing in Taiwan, the mapping report is a statement of capability that adds to route-diversity and repair-access questions rather than a report of any cut.
